# Pop Craft Toolkit: writing a very catchy satirical song about rising p(doom)

This is a practical toolkit. The rules come from songwriting pedagogy (Pat Pattison / Berklee prosody), accounts of Max Martin's "melodic math", earworm research (Jakubowski et al. 2016), and craft analysis of comedy songwriters. Where a source backs a claim, it is cited. Where a rule is craft consensus or my own reasoning, it is labeled as a heuristic. No lyrics from existing songs are reproduced.

---

## 0. The one-sentence strategy

Write a **sincere-sounding, bright, four-chord dance-pop song** whose chorus is **a cheerful chant about the number going up**. Each chorus changes **one word** as the number climbs, and in the final chorus the "fun" framing breaks. The comedy comes from the gap between euphoric pop form and apocalyptic content. Tom Lehrer did this with nuclear war set to a bouncy tune, and it is the core engine here.

## 1. Hook theory

### 1.1 What the earworm research says (Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen, 2016)
The study, in *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity and the Arts*, used about 3,000 people's reports of songs that got stuck in their heads (involuntary musical imagery). It found the following:
- **Faster tempo.** Earworm songs tended to be quicker than comparison songs from the charts. *Rule: target about 110–128 BPM, up-tempo dance-pop.*
- **Common global contour.** A frequent shape rises in pitch through the first phrase and falls in the second (an arch). *Rule: the chorus hook line goes up on the setup and comes down on the title.*
- **A generic, easy melody plus one or two unusual intervals.** The tune is mostly predictable stepwise motion and repetition, with a distinctive leap or an odd repeated interval that sets it apart. *Rule: keep the hook within about a 5th, mostly steps and repeated notes, and add **one** signature leap (for example a jump up a 6th or 7th on a key word, or a falling "sigh").*
- **Popularity and recency also predicted stickiness** (chart success, recent runs). You can't control those, but exposure through repetition inside the song is the lever you do control.

### 1.2 Title placement (craft consensus)
- The title/hook goes in **the first or last line of the chorus, ideally both**. The strongest choice is to open the chorus with it and close the chorus with it (bookending).
- The title lands on **the most rhythmically prominent spot**: the downbeat of the chorus, or the longest held note.
- Heuristic: a listener should hear the title **at least 8–12 times** in a 3-minute song. Hit pop often goes past 20 if you count post-chorus chants.
- Keep the title short: **2–4 syllables**. "p(doom)" sung as "P of DOOM" is 3 syllables with a strong–weak–STRONG pattern (da-da-DUM). That is excellent: it ends on a stressed, long, closed-but-sustainable vowel ("oo").

### 1.3 Max Martin's "melodic math" (reported via collaborators and interviews)
Sources: American Songwriter, Bobby Owsinski, and Musician Wave summaries of the *Songwriters* interviews and profiles.
- **Melody first, lyrics fitted afterward.** Sketch the melody with dummy syllables ("Burger King English"), then find words whose syllables and vowels fit it.
- **Syllable mirroring.** Paired lines have the **same syllable count and the same stress pattern**, so line B is the rhythmic twin of line A. The ear predicts line B and gets rewarded.
- **Pattern, then break.** Repeat a rhythmic cell 2–3 times, then vary the last one (AAAB). The variation is where the payload word goes.
- **Sound beats sense at the margin.** If a slightly less "clever" word sings much better, use the one that sings. Satire complicates this (section 5.4). The rule becomes: find the joke **inside** the singable option, never force an unsingable joke.
- **Each section gets a different rhythmic identity.** For example, verse = busy 16th-note syllables, pre = longer notes and fewer words, chorus = syncopated, held title.

### 1.4 Post-chorus and "drop" hooks
- Modern pop often adds a **post-chorus**: 4–8 bars of a chant, a vocal chop or a nonsense-syllable hook that repeats the title or a fragment of it. It acts as a second hook and doubles hook exposure.
- Techniques include the following:
  - **Stutter/fragment the title**: "P-p-p-p of doom", "doom-doom-doom".
  - **Chant a number**: counting up is naturally catchy and fits the concept perfectly ("ten, twenty, thirty…").
  - **Onomatopoeia instrument hook**: a synth line that plays the exact melody of the title so the title is "heard" even without words.
- Place the post-chorus after chorus 1 and chorus 2, and loop it in the outro.

### 1.5 Nonsense-syllable hooks
- "Oh-oh", "na-na", "la-la", "ay-oh" and "woo" are universal, need no language processing, and invite singalong. Open vowels (ah, oh, ay) sustain and belt well.
- **Satirical upgrade:** make the nonsense syllable *mean* something. "Oo" is already the vowel in "doom", so a crowd-chant "Doo-oo-oom" works as both a nonsense hook and the payload. Similar tricks include "uh-oh" as a nonsense hook that is literally an alarm, and "boom" as a percussive hook that doubles as the joke.

### 1.6 Repetition budget (heuristic)
| Element | Target count per song |
|---|---|
| Title sung | 10–20 |
| Chorus | 3 (last one doubled or extended) |
| Post-chorus chant | 2–3 |
| Signature melodic leap | Once per chorus, same spot |
| Instrumental hook (synth plays title melody) | Intro, post-chorus, outro |

## 2. Prosody (making words sing naturally)

Core principle (Pattison): **stressed syllables on strong beats, unstressed on weak beats.** Keep the natural shape of the spoken language. If a word is mis-stressed when sung (for example "com-PU-ter" sung as "COM-pu-ter"), listeners stumble. If something feels "off", fix the stress before anything else.

### 2.1 Rules
1. **Speak every line out loud in a normal voice, then tap the beat.** The words that naturally get emphasis must land on beats 1 and 3 (or the syncopated anticipations the groove uses).
2. **Long notes need open vowels.** Held notes should use "ah" (sky, night, die, alive), "oh" (go, know, zero, oh no), "ay" (day, AI, stay, away) or "oo" (doom, soon, you, through). Avoid holding short or closed vowels ("it", "bet", "stuck") or consonant clusters ("strengths").
   - Lucky break: **"AI" = "ay-eye"**, two open diphthongs, very singable. **"doom"** = a long "oo" plus a hummable "m" you can sustain.
3. **Consonants at line ends**: plosives (t, k, p) give punch on short staccato notes. Nasals and liquids (m, n, l) can be sustained. "Doom" ending on "m" means you can hum out of it.
4. **Line-length contrast between sections.** Verse lines are long and wordy (8–12 syllables, conversational). Pre-chorus lines are shorter (4–7), with a rising melody. Chorus lines are shortest and most repeated (3–7 syllables, with held notes). Contrast signals "this is the hook".
5. **Rhyme type matches emotional stability** (Pattison). Perfect/family rhymes feel resolved and stable, so use them for chorus payoff lines. Assonance/slant rhymes feel unresolved, so use them for anxious verse lines or the "something's wrong" bridge. **Comedic trick:** make the chorus rhymes smug and perfectly resolved while the content is catastrophe.
6. **Rhyme positions:**
   - **End rhyme**: the anchor.
   - **Internal rhyme**: rhymes inside a line ("the models *scale*, the safeguards *fail*"). This adds density and rhythmic snap and suits verses.
   - **Multisyllabic rhyme**: match 2–3 syllables ("alignment / assignment", "exponential / existential", "benchmark / bench mark"). This is the signature move of dense comedy and hip-hop writing. It delights because it's hard.
   - **Slant rhyme**: "doom / tune / soon / moon / assume / consume" (perfect: room, boom, bloom, zoom, gloom, tomb, whom, costume, perfume, vroom, heirloom, mushroom, loom).
7. **Rhyme scheme per section:** verse AABB or ABAB. Pre-chorus uses a single repeated rhyme to build pressure. Chorus is AAAA or AABA, with the title as the rhyme hub.
8. **Front-load or back-load the payload.** The most important word of a line goes at the **end** (end focus) or on the **first downbeat**. Never bury it mid-line on a weak beat.

### 2.2 Useful singable vocabulary for this topic (rhythm-tagged)
Capitals mark stress.
- p(doom): **P of DOOM** (x / x / pattern: da-da-DUM)
- alignment: a-LIGN-ment. existential: ex-is-TEN-tial. exponential: ex-po-NEN-tial. superintelligence: SU-per-in-TEL-li-gence (awkward, 6 syllables, use sparingly). paperclip: PA-per-clip. compute: com-PUTE. benchmark: BENCH-mark. scaling: SCAL-ing. doomer: DOO-mer. timeline: TIME-line. AGI: A-G-I (three strong syllables, chantable). singularity: sin-gu-LAR-i-ty. shoggoth: SHOG-goth. x-risk: X-risk. RLHF (a clunky initialism, good as a deliberate patter joke).
- Numbers that scan: "ten per CENT" (3), "FIF-ty FIF-ty" (4, trochaic and chantable), "NINE-ty NINE" (3), "one in TEN" (3), "TWEN-ty PER-cent" (4), "HUN-dred" (2). Decimals ("oh point one") are funny as patter. Percent signs read badly in singing, so prefer "percent" or "in ten".

## 3. Section functions (the map)

| Section | Job | Lyric mode | Melody/rhythm | p(doom) application |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Intro** | Plant the instrumental hook | none / chant | Synth plays the title melody | Synth riff equals the "P of DOOM" melody; maybe a spoken sample-like "the current estimate is…" |
| **Verse 1** | Specific detail and story, the "camera" | Concrete nouns, names, scenes, numbers | Lower register, busy syllables, conversational | Scene: a lab/party/podcast where everyone cheerfully quotes their p(doom) like a star sign |
| **Pre-chorus** | Tension, rise, anticipation | Shorter lines, a repeated structure, a question or countdown | Climbs in pitch, rhythm gets sparser, chord hangs on V or IV | The numbers going up: "it was five, then it was ten…" |
| **Chorus** | Simple universal payoff | The title, few words, repeated | Highest sustained notes, arch contour, signature leap | Euphoric, party-anthem framing of rising doom |
| **Post-chorus** | Second hook, chant | Nonsense/fragment/number chant | Rhythmic, repetitive | "Doom-doom-doom", number countdown |
| **Verse 2** | Escalate: new details, raise the stakes, never repeat verse 1's info | New specifics, a bigger joke | Same melody as V1 | Move from lab gossip to world stakes (policy, investors, "ship it anyway") |
| **Bridge** | Perspective flip | Different speaker/POV, sincere moment or reversal | New chords (for example start on vi or bVI), half-time, different rhythm | The AI sings; or the singer admits real fear; or a stripped, sincere confession, which makes the comedy land harder |
| **Final chorus** | Lyrical twist and lift | One changed word or line that reframes everything | Key change up a whole step (truck-driver modulation, deliberately cheesy = satire), gang vocals | The number hits 100 / the chant is now being sung by the machines / "we" becomes "it" |
| **Outro** | Leave the earworm | Post-chorus loop, fade or hard stop | Strip back | Beeping, a synthesized voice finishing the chant, or an abrupt cut to silence (the joke is the ending) |

### Pre-chorus rules
- Lines about half as long as the verse lines.
- Use **anaphora** (repeated line openings) to build pressure: "And they said… / and they said… / and they said…".
- Rising melody and a "lift" into the chorus (a drum fill or a vocal pickup of 1–3 syllables before the downbeat).
- End on an unresolved word or question, or cut off abruptly so the chorus feels like a release.

### Evolving chorus (key device for this concept)
Keep the chorus 90% identical but **change one slot each time**. The numeric slot is ideal:
- Chorus 1: a low number ("ten percent")
- Chorus 2: higher ("fifty-fifty")
- Final chorus: the maximum ("ninety-nine" / "a hundred") or a word replaces the number entirely.
The listener learns the chorus fast (repetition = catchiness) **and** gets a new joke each time (story progression). Same syllable count and stress in the changing slot, always: "ten per CENT" (3) → "FIF-ty FIF" (3) → "NINE-ty NINE" (3). All three are stressed on the last syllable.

## 4. Comedy and satirical pop techniques (techniques only)

### 4.1 Tom Lehrer: cheerful doom and patter density
- **Tonal incongruity:** apocalypse, pollution and war set to jaunty vaudeville, waltz or ragtime. The brighter the music, the darker the lyric can go. This is the primary model for a p(doom) song.
- **Patter/list songs:** fast, even syllables that cram facts into a fixed meter (he set the periodic table to a Gilbert & Sullivan tune). *Technique:* choose a steady 8th- or 16th-note rhythm, and the pleasure comes from technical vocabulary fitting impossibly well. Good for a verse-2 or bridge "rapid fire" list of AI-safety jargon.
- **Deadpan faux-optimism:** the narrator is cheerfully reassuring about the catastrophe ("at least we'll all go together"). The irony is never announced.
- **Precise, erudite rhymes** (multisyllabic, often with technical terms) are themselves jokes.

### 4.2 "Weird Al" Yankovic: joke density and subject mapping
- **Brainstorm exhaustively, then curate.** He's known for generating long lists of every possible joke or angle on a subject, then picking only the best to fit the meter. *Technique:* list 50+ AI-doom specifics (paperclips, "just unplug it", the six-month pause letter, benchmarks, "it's just autocomplete", compute clusters, alignment tax, doomer vs. accelerationist, "stochastic parrot", GPU shortages, "we'll ask the AI how to align the AI") and cast only the best 12 into lines.
- **Commit to a single conceit** and explore every facet of it (a whole song about one food, one hobby, one annoyance).
- **Style parody:** absorb an artist's or genre's idiosyncrasies (production tics, vocal ad-libs, structure), then exaggerate them. Here, the target genre is **euphoric EDM-pop / festival anthem**, with its tics of "hands up", build-ups and "drops".
- **Mundane-vs-epic contrast:** treat trivial things epically or epic things trivially. *Here: treat extinction like a fun party stat.*
- **Every line has a joke, but the chorus has the simplest joke.** Verses carry the dense material, and the chorus is the easy singalong.

### 4.3 Flight of the Conchords: genre pastiche and bathos
- **Perfect genre mimicry** (production and vocal delivery are dead serious), with lyrics that deflate. The sincere delivery makes the joke.
- **Bathos:** build a grand line and end on something banal. *Technique:* grand setup ("as the silicon gods awaken…") → banal payoff ("…I should probably update my LinkedIn").
- **Overly literal / naive narrator:** the singer misunderstands the stakes or takes a metaphor literally.
- **Awkward specificity:** hyper-specific, unglamorous details are funnier than generic ones.

### 4.4 Bo Burnham: escalation, meta and tonal turn
- **Escalating tempo/intensity:** a song starts as a cheerful sales pitch and accelerates into manic menace. The *form* enacts the content. *Here: tempo or density rising along with p(doom).*
- **Meta-awareness:** a song that comments on itself or on the pop form ("this is the part where the key changes"). Use at most once. It's a spice.
- **The sincere turn:** a comedy song drops the irony for one moment of real feeling, usually in the bridge, then snaps back. This makes the audience care, and then the final joke hits harder.
- **Voice as character:** the narrator is a persona (a salesman, an influencer, a corporate host). *Here: an upbeat AI-company hype persona, or the AI itself as a friendly assistant.*

### 4.5 General comedy-lyric mechanics
- **Rule of three:** two normal items, the third subverts ("we've got guardrails, we've got red teams, we've got vibes").
- **Callbacks:** a detail from verse 1 returns transformed in the final chorus or outro (for example, the verse-1 party guest who said "ten percent" is heard in the outro saying "a hundred").
- **Escalation:** each verse raises the stakes by an order of magnitude.
- **Double meanings / puns** that work in *both* readings at once. The singable test is that the line must make sense even if the listener misses the pun. Candidates: "model" (AI / fashion / role model), "weights" (neural / gym / burden), "training" (ML / gym), "alignment" (AI / chiropractor / stars), "tokens" (LLM / arcade / gesture), "scale" (compute / fish / music scale / weighing), "loss" (loss function / grief), "attention" (transformers / needy lover), "prompt" (input / punctual), "hallucinate" (AI / party drugs), "run" (execute / flee), "cloud" (compute / weather / doom), "boom" (economic boom / explosion), "rise of the machines" (market / apocalypse), "bullish".
- **Understatement of the catastrophic** ("slight change of plans") and **overstatement of the trivial**.
- **Specific > general, always.** "Four hundred thousand GPUs" beats "lots of computers".
- **The listener must get the joke at tempo.** At 120 BPM each line flies by. Put the punch word at the **end** of the line on a held note or right before a rest, so it has time to land. Leave a beat of space (a rest or instrumental stab) after big jokes. That is the "laugh gap".

## 5. Packing meaning densely without losing singability

1. **Concrete nouns over abstractions.** "Server farm", "red button", "paperclip", "kill switch", "bunker in New Zealand" rather than "danger", "technology", "the future".
2. **Numbers that scan** (see 2.2). Numbers are concrete, funny when precise, and let the chorus evolve. Prefer 1–3-syllable numbers with end stress for held notes.
3. **Name-drops of concepts and memes, not real people.** Use genre-recognizable references (paperclip maximizer, Turing test, "stochastic parrot", the pause letter, "just one more scaling run"). Avoid naming real private individuals. Public-concept references age better and avoid targeting anyone.
4. **Callbacks** reward attention and cost zero new setup.
5. **The evolving chorus** tells a story using just one changing word.
6. **Double-duty lines:** every verse line should (a) advance the story, (b) carry a joke or specific image, **and** (c) rhyme or scan into the pattern. If a line only does one, cut or rewrite it.
7. **Density lives in verses; air lives in choruses.** Verse lines can be 10–14 syllables of patter. Chorus lines have 3–7 syllables with held vowels. Never cram the chorus.
8. **Compress with the right word class:** strong verbs ("scales", "ships", "spawns") beat adverb + weak verb. Cut articles and filler ("just", "really", "so") unless the rhythm needs a weak syllable.
9. **Initialisms as rhythm:** "A-G-I", "G-P-U" give three strong syllables each. Chant-worthy.
10. **Borrowed forms:** a countdown, a weather forecast, a flight-safety announcement, a product launch, a horoscope ("what's your p(doom)?" as a pickup line). A borrowed frame gives instant structure for jokes.
11. **Contrast the speaker's cheerfulness with the content** so every line gets irony "for free".

### 5.4 Resolving the "sense vs. sound" conflict (satire-specific)
Max Martin says sound wins. Satire needs the joke. Proceed in this order:
1. Lock the melody and syllable/stress grid first.
2. Brainstorm 5–10 jokes that could fill each slot.
3. Keep only jokes that fit the grid **naturally stressed**.
4. If the best joke won't fit, move it to a section with a different grid (for example the patter verse or the bridge) rather than mangling the chorus.

## 6. Title and concept bank for p(doom)

- The title or chorus hook centered on **"P of Doom"** (3 syllables, ends on a sustainable "oo" + "m").
- Rhyme bank on "-oom": boom, zoom, room, bloom, gloom, tomb, loom, vroom, broom, groom, whom, costume, perfume, heirloom, mushroom, resume, consume, assume, presume, "cloud of doom", "ka-boom", "va-va-voom".
- Slant: soon, tune, moon, June, noon, balloon, afternoon, immune, true, through, you, two, new, "too soon".
- Comedic frames: p(doom) as a **dating profile stat** / small-talk icebreaker; as a **stock ticker that only goes up** ("bullish on the apocalypse"); as a **party chant** (the drop IS the doom); as a **weather forecast** ("ninety percent chance of…").
- Signature leap idea: jump up on "DOOM" (for example a 6th above "of"), then descend stepwise. That's the arch contour plus one unusual interval, per Jakubowski.

## 7. Line-scoring checklist

Score each line 0/1 on every item. Rewrite any **verse** line under 7/10 and any **chorus** line under 9/10. Items marked (C) are chorus-critical.

**Singability**
1. (C) **Stress match:** spoken stress lands on the strong beats (say it, tap it).
2. (C) **Vowel fit:** every held note sits on an open vowel (ah / oh / ay / oo / ee), with no consonant clusters on long notes.
3. **Syllable mirror:** the syllable count and stress pattern match the line's partner (melodic math), or it deliberately breaks the pattern at the payload.
4. **Breathable:** it can be sung at tempo without a gasp, with a natural rest before the next line.

**Hook and structure**
5. (C) **Title proximity:** in the chorus, the title is in this line or the adjacent one, and the title sits on the strongest beat or longest note.
6. **Section-appropriate length:** long for verse, short for pre, shortest for chorus.
7. **Rhyme:** it lands a rhyme of the right stability (perfect for resolution, slant for unease), with a bonus for internal or multisyllabic rhyme.

**Meaning and comedy**
8. **Concrete:** it contains at least one specific noun, number or image (no pure abstraction).
9. **Double duty:** it advances the story **and** carries a joke, pun or ironic contrast.
10. **Punch placement:** the funniest word is last or on a downbeat, followed by space.
11. **Clarity at tempo:** a first-time listener gets it at full speed (no read-only wordplay or jargon that needs a footnote, unless the obscurity *is* the joke in a patter section).
12. (C) **Tonal irony intact:** the delivery stays cheerful or sincere while the content is dark. The song never winks too hard or explains the joke.

**Whole-song pass (run once)**
- Title heard at least 10 times? Post-chorus exists?
- Evolving chorus slot has the same syllables and stress each time?
- Verse 2 escalates rather than repeats verse 1?
- Bridge flips perspective (POV, sincerity or speaker)?
- Final chorus has a twist word and a lift (key change / gang vocals)?
- At least one callback from verse 1 pays off at the end?
- Tempo about 110–128 BPM, chorus melody arch-shaped with one signature leap?
- No lyrics borrowed from existing songs; the melody isn't lifted from a known tune (check by humming the hook for others)?
